Should the team build it on a publish/subscribe fan-out model (each consumer group sees all events, easy to add new consumers, at-least-once semantics to manage), or a durable point-to-point work queue (one consumer per message, simpler back-pressure and ordering, harder to add new independent consumers later)?

- **Defence position:** The Defence will defend the publish/subscribe fan-out model, asserting that its inherent scalability through easy addition of new consumer groups and native fan-out capabilities outweigh the complexities of managing at-least-once semantics.
- **Prosecution position:** The Prosecution will argue for the durable point-to-point work queue, contending that its deterministic message delivery, simplified back-pressure handling, and strict ordering guarantees are essential to the system’s reliability despite reduced consumer scalability.

### Opening statements

**DEFENCE — opening statement**

In a high‑volume data‑processing pipeline, the publish/subscribe fan‑out model is the superior architectural choice because it permits effortless horizontal scaling by simply adding new consumer groups without disrupting existing workloads or reconfiguring routing logic. The decoupled nature of pub/sub ensures that each consumer group processes events independently, enabling rapid experimentation, A/B testing, and feature toggling while maintaining a single source of truth for event emission. While at‑least‑once semantics do introduce replay scenarios, the complexity is mitigated by standard idempotent handling patterns and lightweight offset tracking, allowing teams to focus on core business logic rather than custom queue orchestration. Moreover, the inherent fan‑out capability eliminates the need for manual work‑queue sharding or leader election, reducing operational overhead and simplifying observability across the entire data pipeline. Consequently, the publish/subscribe approach delivers superior scalability, resilience, and agility compared to a durable point‑to‑point work queue that would otherwise impose rigid consumer limits and cumbersome back‑pressure mechanics.

_Key points:_
- Easy horizontal scaling via addition of new consumer groups
- Independent processing enables rapid experimentation and feature toggling
- Standard idempotence and offset tracking handle at‑least‑once semantics efficiently
- Reduced operational overhead: no sharding, leader election or complex back‑pressure


**PROSECUTION — opening statement**

The Prosecution submits that the durable point-to-point work queue is the optimal architecture for this data-processing pipeline due to its inherent advantages in message delivery reliability, back-pressure management, and ordering guarantees. Unlike the publish/subscribe model, which risks at-least-once semantics leading to potential duplicates and complicates state management, the durable point-to-point approach ensures each message is delivered exactly once, preserving data integrity. This architecture simplifies back-pressure handling by allowing consumers to process messages at their own pace without overloading the system, while also maintaining strict ordering of operations, which is critical for transactional consistency. Although it may be more challenging to scale horizontally by adding new independent consumers later, the deterministic and reliable nature of message delivery ensures a robust and predictable system that minimizes errors and reprocessing overhead.

_Key points:_
- Deterministic message delivery ensures each message is processed exactly once, avoiding duplicates and maintaining data integrity.
- Simplified back-pressure handling allows consumers to manage their workload independently, preventing system overload and improving efficiency.
- Strict ordering guarantees maintain the sequence of operations, ensuring transactional consistency and reducing potential conflicts.
- The architecture's reliability outweighs the trade-off of reduced scalability when considering long-term system stability and operational predictability.

2️⃣ **Schema evolution in point‑to‑point queues** – Kafka’s Schema Registry keeps every schema version for a topic, enabling consumers to specify the exact version they understand. A consumer group that needs legacy data simply selects the older version; new groups automatically receive the latest version. No duplication of data or sharding is required because all versions coexist within the same topic while being isolated by consumer‑specific serializers. A point‑to‑point queue would need either a separate physical queue per schema or a custom routing layer that duplicates records to multiple queues, which increases storage and violates isolation guarantees – an unnecessary complication for systems at 100k+ consumers.

3️⃣ **Performance under back‑pressure** – At‑least‑once semantics in Kafka mean that if a consumer lags, the broker retains all messages until acknowledged. The system can still ingest new events at full throughput because production is never blocked by slow consumers; instead, lagging groups are merely read slower. Retry logic is implemented on the consumer side, allowing fine‑grained back‑pressure controls such as pause/resume or commit‑per‑batch strategies. Point‑to‑point queues with strict ordering require the broker to hold a single message until the current consumer acknowledges it before delivering the next, creating a natural bottleneck and forcing synchronous processing across all consumers. In high‑throughput scenarios this serializes traffic and inflates latency, whereas pub/sub keeps throughput high while individual groups can throttle independently.

4️⃣ **Team expertise trade‑offs** – Kafka’s ecosystem (Kafka Streams, ksqlDB, Connect, MirrorMaker) gives teams out‑of‑the‑box operators for monitoring, data transformation, and integration. The learning curve is largely about understanding partitioning and consumer group semantics, not building a custom queue stack. Building and maintaining a durable point‑to‑point infrastructure would involve designing a broker, client libraries, persistence layer, dead‑letter handling, and compliance logging – an effort that consumes significant engineering time and introduces hidden technical debt.

**Answer to prosecution’s question on selective rollback:** In Kafka you can create separate logical topics or consumer groups per service version. When a backward‑incompatible schema is deployed, only the affected consumer group is paused, its offsets reset, and it begins consuming from the new topic that uses the updated schema. Other consumer groups continue reading from the old topic unchanged, so their processing pipeline remains unaffected. Kafka’s offset management guarantees that each group can replay or skip records independently without impacting others, a feature that would require custom sharding logic and duplicated queues in a point‑to‑point system.

**New argument – decoupled evolution** – By using a fan‑out model every downstream microservice becomes an independent subscriber. This architectural decoupling means new features or bug fixes can be rolled out to a subset of consumers without touching the upstream publisher or other services. In contrast, a point‑to‑point queue tightly couples producers and consumers; any change in consumer logic forces a system‑wide deployment to avoid blocking the queue, creating higher risk for coordinated releases.

### Closing statements

**DEFENCE — closing statement**

In closing, the evidence overwhelmingly supports the publish/subscribe fan‑out model as the superior architectural choice for a high‑volume data‑processing pipeline. First, its horizontal scalability—adding new consumer groups simply requires subscription without rebalancing an existing queue—enables zero‑downtime upgrades and feature rollouts that would otherwise demand costly broker‑wide migrations in a point‑to‑point setup. Second, the fan‑out architecture isolates each consumer’s processing logic, providing natural blast‑radius containment, fine‑grained ACLs, and per‑topic encryption that reduce attack surface while keeping operational overhead low; in contrast, a monolithic queue forces every consumer to share a single ordering space and a shared back‑pressure path, complicating both security and failure isolation. Third, the use of a centralized schema registry with backward‑compatibility rules guarantees that divergent schema versions can coexist without risking data inconsistency, whereas point‑to‑point queues would need bespoke sharding or duplicate queues to achieve similar safety. Finally, the proven ability to maintain at‑least‑once semantics through idempotent processing patterns and lightweight offset tracking shows that the perceived risk of duplication is negligible compared with the tangible benefits in scalability, resilience, and observability that pub/sub delivers. Thus, the court should rule in favor of a fan‑out model, recognizing that its design choices directly address the operational realities and long‑term growth demands far more effectively than any deterministic point‑to‑point alternative.

- Linear scaling through independent consumer groups eliminates rebalancing bottlenecks
- Built‑in isolation delivers robust blast‑radius containment and fine‑grained security controls
- Centralized schema registry with backward‑compatibility preserves consistency across evolving schemas

Furthermore, strict ordering guarantees enhance reliability, making it essential for systems dependent on precise sequence execution. While the publish/subscribe model offers scalability, the operational efficiency and reliability of point-to-point queues provide a more robust solution, particularly in scenarios where data consistency and order are non-negotiable.
